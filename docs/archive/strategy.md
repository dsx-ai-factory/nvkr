NVIDIA K8s Definition* (NKD)
*not a distro.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Historical product-strategy input.** This document is not a current implementation, support, or release
> claim. The proposed ADRs and `docs/adr/reconciliation.md` govern the reviewed engineering baseline.


NKD provides baseline K8s for AI Factories. 

1. Who is the customer?
The customer is the AI factory operator. The team running GPU infrastructure needs to offer self-service Kubernetes to their tenants, where tenants sit outside the operator's group either as internal business units or external paying customers.
2. What is the customer problem or opportunity?
NVIDIA Kubernetes Engine (NKE) instantiates a management plane and workload Kubernetes clusters for operators and tenants within the AI Factory. To do this, NKE needs Kubernetes software to run. This software must be a specific configuration and version to ensure that it works. Software includes: 
NKE Management controllers
Kubernetes control plane components
Kubernetes data plane components
Kubernetes runtime components (both non-accelerated/core and accelerated)
Today, a customer will have to understand the requirements of the NKE management software shipped by NVIDIA, download Kubernetes source code, configure and build it as multiple independent disk images and containers before they can install NKE. They will also need to keep track of patches and configuration changes across multiple projects over time to ensure NKE and the clusters it instantiates remain secure and functional over time. Our mission is to enable AI Factories to stand up a reliable managed Kubernetes service in days. Without guidance on which Kubernetes software to run, customers are forced to do extensive work and testing, either from upstream or an existing third-party distro, then ensure that distro remains compatible with NKE.
3. What is the most important customer benefit?
The most important benefit is that an operator can produce a Kubernetes build NKE will accept, in their own registry and on their own OS images, in days instead of the weeks or months it takes to reverse-engineer that build from upstream. NKD tells the customer exactly which Kubernetes components, versions, patches, and configuration NKE requires, and gives them a pipeline that turns that specification into the disk images and containers NKE consumes. The customer runs the pipeline. NVIDIA owns the specification.
The thing customers actually lack is not Kubernetes binaries. Upstream is free, and most operators already run a distro or can pull one. What they lack is the answer to which configuration NKE was built and tested against, and the assurance that the answer stays correct as CVEs land and versions move. Ship a distro and NVIDIA takes ownership of every customer's registry choice, airgap posture, kernel version, and golden-image format, plus CNCF conformance submission and a CVE SLA on binaries we had no need to build. Ship a recipe and a build pipeline and the customer keeps their environment, their supply chain, and their patch cadence, while NVIDIA owns the one thing only NVIDIA can: the validated configuration that makes NKE and the accelerated runtime work together.
The pipeline takes the NKD specification and produces the same qcow images and container artifacts NKE expects, reproducibly, so that when NVIDIA publishes a patched control-plane version or a new GPU Operator pairing, the customer re-runs the pipeline rather than re-doing the integration. Days to stand up, and a repeatable path to stay current, without the customer inheriting a distro that NVIDIA is now on the hook to maintain for them.
4. How do you know what customers need or want?
We know because our first and most demanding customers are internal, in production, and already hitting the build-and-maintain wall described above. DSX is pulling this directly. Cam Moore: "I want a high quality K8s I can point at for DSX OS." That is a customer asking for the product by name, not a hypothesis we are testing.
The internal NKE fleet runs 17,000+ GPUs across 42 clusters and 22 sites, and every one of those clusters runs a Kubernetes someone had to curate, build, patch, and keep compatible with NKE. The machinery that took already exists because it had to: a GPG-signed apt repo, per-artifact SBOMs, an OS train shipping regular dated releases with embedded CVE scans and CIS hardening. Every NCP operator inherits this cost the day they stand NKE up themselves.
The NCP Market Survey refreshed in May 2026 and the K8aaS requirements both point at operators who need to offer managed Kubernetes on GPUs and have no sanctioned answer for which Kubernetes to run. The NKE architecture review names the gaps operators raise when they try upstream: supply-chain control so CVE fixes ship fast, published Kubernetes and AI conformance, open validation test suites, qualified worker OS images that vend kernel and kubelet CVE fixes, and confidential computing for tenants who will not run proprietary models without it. Those are the questions operators are already asking, and today the answer is "build it yourself."
5. What does the customer experience look like? 
From NVIDIA the operator gets three things: a recipe that pins which Kubernetes version and components to run, the tools that turn that recipe into deployable images, and NKE itself, the engine that installs and operates the clusters. The operator brings the rest from their own environment: a base Ubuntu image, their container registry, their secret store, and the bare-metal or VM capacity the clusters run on.
First the operator builds the images. They run the build tools against the recipe, and the tools produce two artifacts: a bootable OS image and the matching set of Kubernetes packages. Both land in the operator's own registry. This is the step that used to mean downloading Kubernetes source and working out by hand which versions and patches NKE needs. Now the recipe answers that and the tools do the work.
Then the operator installs NKE. A short, documented bootstrap stands up the Site Controller, the small cluster that runs NKE's engine, by booting its machines from the image the operator just built. NKE installs Kubernetes from the packages. It does not build Kubernetes, which is why the images have to exist first.
Now the operator or tenants can provision clusters using NKE's API, and every cluster is built from the same recipe and images. A tenant asks for a cluster by intent, training on H100 at the current version, and the cluster is provisioned and configured for them. Upgrades keep the infrastructure immutable: NKE builds a new image from an updated recipe and rolls it out by replacing nodes around live workloads, never by patching them in place. A node is replaced, not mutated, so the fleet always matches the recipe.
6. What is the solution you’re proposing?
NKD is a recipe and a build pipeline, not a shipped Kubernetes distribution. NVIDIA gives the operator the recipe that pins the tested Kubernetes version and components, and the tools that turn it into a bootable OS image and matching packages. The operator runs those tools and owns the output. We are deciding, for now, not to ship built binaries or a pre-assembled distro. We tell operators how to run Kubernetes for NKE, and they build it in their own environment.
The pieces are mostly already built. The recipe is expressed in AICR, the runtime layer that assembles the GPU and Network operators and pulls from NKD by default. The image is built by Skysmith, which bakes the recipe into an ISO in CI and emits a CycloneDX SBOM. A package builder assembles the version-pinned Kubernetes packages. NodeWright is the workload-aware rollout engine for immutable upgrades and the CVE fast-path. NKE, the engine, consumes that output at bootstrap and reuses it for every cluster.
Shipping a built distribution would put NVIDIA on the hook for every operator's registry, kernel version, and image format, plus CNCF conformance submission and a CVE SLA on binaries we had no need to build. The scarce thing is not the binaries, which are free from upstream. It is the validated configuration that makes NKE and the accelerated runtime agree, and the pipeline that reproduces it as CVEs land and versions move. NKD ships those two things and leaves the operator's environment intact.
The design bet underneath is that AI clouds should run like clouds, not data centers. Data centers treat nodes as long-lived hosts patched in place, which is why GPU fleets drift to many versions and sit a year unpatched. Cloud treats the image as the unit of version and replaces nodes without disrupting workloads. NKD builds for the second model. The recipe is what makes that immutability reproducible in someone else's environment: the same recipe produces the same image every time, and every node either matches it or is replaced.
7. How will you measure success?
P0: Customers can successfully start NKE without contacting NVIDIA directly.
8. How does DS9 use NKD internally?
DS9 is the first operator, and it uses NKD exactly the way an external NCP will. The SRE team that runs DS9 takes the same recipe and the same build tools, produces the same OS image and packages in DS9's own registry, and bootstraps NKE from them. There is no privileged internal path and no internal-only artifact. What DS9 builds is what an NCP builds.
9. Are we shipping built Kubernetes binaries or a pre-assembled distribution?
No. NKD ships as a recipe and a build pipeline, and the operator runs the pipeline to produce the OS image and packages in their own registry. NVIDIA validates the recipe and maintains the tools. The operator owns the built artifacts and where they live.
Shipping built binaries would make NVIDIA responsible for every operator's registry, kernel version, image format, CVE-response SLA, and CNCF conformance submission, for artifacts the operator can reproduce from a recipe we already validate. The upstream binaries are free. The value sits in the validated configuration and the pipeline that rebuilds it deterministically as CVEs land and versions move, so NKD ships those.
This is a deliberate scoping decision for v1, and it diverges from earlier framing that treated NKD as a shipped nke-distro with its own CNCF-certified target. If operators cannot or will not run the pipeline, publishing reference images becomes a fast follow. The recipe and tools remain the product, and reference images would sit on top as a convenience.
10. How do determinism and immutability work together in NKD? Why are they important?
Determinism means the pipeline reproduces the same artifacts from the same recipe. The recipe pins every Kubernetes version, package, and hash, and the build emits a byte-deterministic CycloneDX SBOM, so any operator rebuilding from a given recipe gets an identical OS image and package bundle. The recipe becomes the single source of truth for what a node contains.
Immutability means every change to a node arrives as a new image. The image is the unit of version. To move a node forward, the operator builds from an updated recipe and reimages the node, bounded by an interruption budget so running jobs hold their nodes until they drain. A running node is never edited in place.
The two properties hold each other up. Immutability guarantees a node matches the image it booted from. Determinism guarantees that image matches the recipe and can be rebuilt and audited to the byte. Together they let an operator prove that what runs in production equals what the recipe specifies, and check it against the SBOM at any time. DGXC lacked both: nodes were patched by hand, builds were not reproducible, and the fleet drifted to many versions with no reliable record of what was installed.
The CVE fast-path is the one exception. When a fix cannot wait for a full reimage, NodeWright applies it to live nodes, and the same fix folds into the next recipe so the following build restores determinism.
11. What should we call this?
NKD stands for NVIDIA Kubernetes Definition. The acronym stays, so the strategy doc and the nke-distro repo keep their references, and the expansion now describes the product accurately: a declarative, version-pinned, deterministic definition of the Kubernetes NKE runs, plus the pipeline that realizes it.
"Distribution" was the wrong word because it promises a shipped, curated binary bundle in the mold of RKE2, k3s, or OpenShift, with maintained artifacts and a CNCF badge on the box. NKD hands over a recipe and a build pipeline and has the operator produce the artifacts. "Definition" matches the spine of the proposal, where the recipe is the single source of truth and every node either matches it or gets replaced.
We considered NVIDIA Kubernetes Blueprint, NVIDIA Kubernetes Recipe, and folding the deliverable into NKE as a reference build. Blueprint and Recipe both cost the NKD token, and Recipe collides with AICR's recipe layer. Keeping NKD as NVIDIA Kubernetes Definition fixes the meaning at the lowest churn.
